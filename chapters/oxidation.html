Thermal Oxidation, Isolation & Gate Dielectrics

In PMIC and BCD integration, silicon dioxide can serve as a gate dielectric, field isolation or process mask. Its thickness, interface quality and thermal history influence device operation and later junction profiles. This chapter introduces thermal growth with an educational Deal–Grove model, optical thickness cues, local oxidation and oxide charge. Model values are illustrative and are not foundry recipes.

  • Use the Deal–Grove relation to distinguish reaction-limited and diffusion-limited growth.

Oxide Growth at the Silicon Interface

Thermal oxidation forms silicon dioxide by heating a silicon wafer in an oxidizing ambient, commonly in a furnace or rapid thermal system. Oxygen is used for dry oxidation; water vapor is used for wet oxidation. The choice affects growth rate and oxide/interface properties.

$$\mathrm{Si + O_2 \rightarrow SiO_2}\quad(\text{dry}), \qquad \mathrm{Si + 2H_2O \rightarrow SiO_2 + 2H_2}\quad(\text{wet})$$

The reaction occurs at the silicon/oxide interface. Oxidant diffuses through the existing oxide before reacting with silicon. Growth consumes part of the substrate and expands partly above the original surface. In BCD integration, this affects junction depth, isolation geometry and the interface beneath a gate dielectric.

Deal–Grove model

In 1965, Bruce Deal and Andrew Grove modeled oxidation as a steady-state process involving three fluxes. The model balances three coupled fluxes: gas-to-surface transport \(F_1\), diffusion through the oxide \(F_2\), and reaction at the silicon interface \(F_3\).

$$F_1 = h\,(C^* - C_0), \qquad F_2 = D\,\frac{C_0 - C_i}{x}, \qquad F_3 = k_s\,C_i$$
\(C^*\) is the equilibrium oxidant concentration at the oxide surface (Henry's law, proportional to partial pressure), while \(C_0\) and \(C_i\) are the concentrations at the oxide surface and silicon interface. \(D\) is the diffusion coefficient in the oxide and \(k_s\) is the interface reaction-rate constant. At steady state, \(F_1 = F_2 = F_3 = F\).

Combining the three flux equations, eliminating \(C_i\), and integrating the growth rate \(dx/dt = F/N_1\) gives the linear–parabolic relation. Here \(N_1\) is the number of oxidant molecules incorporated per unit oxide volume (approximately 2.2×10²² cm⁻³ for dry oxide and 4.4×10²² cm⁻³ for wet oxide).

$$x^2 + A\,x = B\,(t + \tau), \qquad B = \frac{2DC^*}{N_1}, \qquad \frac{B}{A} = \frac{C^*}{N_1}\cdot\frac{k_s h}{k_s + h} \approx \frac{k_sC^*}{N_1}$$
\(B\) is a parabolic rate constant (diffusion), and \(B/A\) is a linear rate constant (reaction). \(\tau\) is a time shift reflecting the initial thickness \(x_i\): \(\tau = (x_i^2 + Ax_i)/B\). Both constants are of the Arrhenius type \(C\,e^{-E_a/kT}\), and the activation energy of \(B\) corresponds to diffusion in the oxide (dry 1.23 eV, wet 0.71~0.78 eV), and \(B/A\) corresponds to Si–Si bond breaking (approximately 2.0 eV).

For a thin film (\(x \ll A\)), growth is approximately linear and limited by interface reaction: \(x \approx (B/A)t\). For a thick film (\(x \gg A\)), diffusion through the oxide limits growth: \(x \approx \sqrt{Bt}\). Doubling thickness then takes roughly four times as long.

Limits of the Deal–Grove approximation for thin oxides

Deal–Grove can miss the nonlinear initial stage of dry oxidation; this source describes the first ~30 nm as an anomalous thin-oxide regime and discusses an initial-thickness \(\tau\) correction and the Massoud model. Do not use this simplified fit to select a gate dielectric. Very thin gate stacks are often discussed using equivalent oxide thickness (EOT), which is an electrical equivalence and not necessarily the physical film thickness. Gate dielectrics depend on device and platform: one published 0.18 µm CMOS-compatible BCD LDMOS study reports an 11.5 nm gate oxide. That result is one measured device example, not a general BCD rule. Oxidation methods and thickness targets must be chosen for the specific structure and process.

Oxide Thickness and Optical Interference

Color changes with oxide thickness because light reflected at the air/oxide and oxide/silicon interfaces interferes. The round-trip optical path difference \(2nx\) produces constructive or destructive interference depending on wavelength. Color is an approximate visual cue; ellipsometry and reflectance spectroscopy (Chapter 20) measure the same underlying optical response more systematically.

$$r = \frac{r_{01} + r_{12}\,e^{-2i\delta}}{1 + r_{01}r_{12}\,e^{-2i\delta}}, \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi n_1 x}{\lambda}, \qquad R = |r|^2$$
\(r_{01}\) is the air/oxide, \(r_{12}\) is the Fresnel reflection coefficient (normal incidence) of the oxide/silicon interface, and \(n_1 \approx 1.46\) is the refractive index of the oxide. The complex refractive index of silicon varies with wavelength (about 5.6 − 0.39i at 400 nm, about 3.9 − 0.02i at 600 nm).

LOCOS, Bird’s-Beak Encroachment and Trench Isolation

To electrically separate transistors, a thick insulating film is needed between them. The standard from the 1970s to the 1990s was LOCOS(LOCal Oxidation of Silicon). A thin pad oxide and nitride film are applied on top of the silicon, and wet oxidation is performed after leaving the nitride film only where the device will be inserted. Since the nitride film barely allows oxidizing agents to pass through, a thick field oxide grows only in exposed areas.

The problem is the edges. The oxidant penetrates the thin pad oxide laterally underneath the nitride film, and the silicon there is also oxidized, increasing in volume and lifting the end of the nitride film. Because the cross section resembles a bird's beak, it is called Bird's beak. The lateral encroachment consumes active area, which becomes increasingly costly as device dimensions shrink.

Since the late 1990s, LOCOS has been replaced by STI(Shallow Trench Isolation). This method involves digging a shallow trench in silicon, filling it with a CVD oxide, and then flattening it with CMP, so there are no bird’s-beaks and the surface is flat. Oxidation is still used in the step of growing a thin thermal oxide (liner) on the trench walls. STI is covered in Chapter 18.

Oxide Charge, Interfaces and Device Behavior

SiO₂ is not a perfect insulator but carries several types of charges. These charges shift the transistor's threshold voltage and reduce its reliability, so they are managed by oxidizing conditions and subsequent heat treatment.

Oxidation also affects the dopants in silicon. At the interface, the dopant is redistributed to favor the oxide or silicon (segregation coefficient m = CSi/CSiO₂). Boron (m ≈ 0.3) partitions into the oxide, lowering its surface concentration, and phosphorus and arsenic (m ≈ 10) are pushed out of the oxide and accumulate at the interface (pile-up). Also, during oxidation, atoms between the silicon lattice pour out from the interface, causing rapid diffusion of boron and phosphorus. This is oxidation-accelerated diffusion(OED), and is revisited in Chapter 15.

Dielectric choice and thermal budget

In BCD, gate and field dielectrics must meet different electrical and isolation needs. Their formation also adds thermal exposure that can redistribute wells, drift implants and junctions. High-k/metal-gate stacks are technology-dependent options rather than a universal BCD requirement; evaluate dielectric choices together with interface quality, leakage, breakdown and the process thermal budget (Chapter 23).

Key Takeaways

  1. Oxidant diffuses through the oxide and reacts at the silicon interface. Forming oxide of thickness \(x\) consumes approximately \(0.44x\) of silicon.
  2. The Deal–Grove relation \(x^2 + Ax = B(t+\tau)\) describes near-linear reaction-limited growth for thin films and diffusion-limited growth for thicker films.
  3. Wet oxidation generally grows faster than dry oxidation. Growth coefficients also depend on temperature, orientation, pressure and technology.
  4. Interference color provides a qualitative thickness cue; ellipsometry uses related optical principles for measurement.
  5. LOCOS can form lateral bird’s-beak encroachment beneath the mask edge. Trench isolation provides a different, more scalable isolation geometry in many processes.

Knowledge Check

How much silicon is consumed when growing 200 nm of oxide?

0.44 × 200 ≈ 88 nm. The remaining 112 nm rises above the original surface.

In the diffusion-limited regime, what happens to oxide thickness if oxidation time is quadrupled?

In the diffusion-limited regime, \(x \approx \sqrt{Bt}\), so quadrupling time approximately doubles thickness.

Why is wet oxidation generally faster than dry oxidation?

At 1000 °C, the \(C^*\) of H₂O is about 3×10¹⁹ cm⁻³, which is hundreds of times that of O₂ (about 5×10¹⁶). The diffusion coefficient is rather similar or smaller.

What are the conditions for the bird’s-beak to become longer in LOCOS?

The pad oxide is a passage through which the oxidant passes sideways underneath the nitride film. The thicker the passage, the easier it is to oxidize laterally.
