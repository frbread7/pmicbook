Chapter 16 · Surface planarization

Chemical mechanical planarization (CMP)

CMP removes topography by combining pad contact, slurry chemistry and mechanical motion. PMIC layouts contain dense power devices, isolation, contacts and thick interconnect regions beside lower-density areas. Pattern-dependent removal can leave residual steps, dishing or erosion that affect lithography, contact formation, resistance and reliability.

  • Explain the qualitative role of pressure, relative velocity and slurry in removal.
  • Relate pattern density and feature width to nonuniform planarization.
  • Recognize dishing, erosion and residual topography.
  • Explain how layout density and dummy fill can reduce local variation.

Pad, slurry and pressure

A common empirical description is Preston’s relation: removal rate varies approximately with pressure and relative velocity over a calibrated operating window. Slurry chemistry and pad state influence the proportionality constant and selectivity. This relationship is useful for intuition, but it is not a universal physical law or an equipment recipe.

$$R=k_P\,P\,v$$
The Preston coefficient \(k_P\) depends on pad, slurry, material, conditioning and process window. The interactive models use normalized or representative rates only.

Pattern density and local planarization

Dense regions can carry more pad contact and remove exposed material differently from sparse regions. The resulting local step-height variation is sometimes called pattern-dependent planarization. In a BCD flow, isolation topography, contact arrays and device density may vary across the die, so both local and wafer-scale uniformity matter.

Metal dishing and dielectric erosion

When a wide metal feature is polished, the pad may deflect into the feature and remove metal below the surrounding dielectric plane: dishing. In dense arrays, the field dielectric can also recess relative to the local surface: erosion. Both reduce dimensional margin and can alter line resistance or dielectric thickness.

Integration consequence

PMIC power paths rely on low-resistance metal and robust dielectric separation. Recess, thickness loss or nonuniformity can change resistance, capacitance and reliability margin; electrical characterization is required to quantify impact.

Planarization length and layout density

Longer-wavelength pattern-density changes can survive polishing even when short-range topography is removed. Dummy fill adds nonfunctional features to balance density and improve process uniformity, subject to electrical, thermal and design-rule constraints.

Defects and post-CMP cleaning

Common CMP concerns include scratches, particles, slurry residue, corrosion, dishing, erosion and delamination. Cleaning and inspection follow polishing, and the acceptance criteria depend on the material stack and downstream process. In PMIC interconnects, inspect both geometric variation and electrical consequences such as line resistance or leakage.

Key points

  1. CMP combines chemistry, pad mechanics and relative motion; Preston’s relation is an empirical approximation.
  2. Pattern density and feature dimensions affect local removal and residual topography.
  3. Dishing lowers wide metal features; erosion recesses surrounding dielectric in dense patterns.
  4. Dummy fill can improve density balance but must obey design and electrical constraints.
  5. Educational models show trends only; calibrate process behavior with measurements.

Knowledge check

What is metal dishing?

Pad deflection and material removal can recess broad metal features.

Why can layout dummy fill help CMP?

Density balancing can help, but fill has to meet layout and circuit constraints.

What does Preston’s relation provide?

The proportionality coefficient depends on materials and process conditions.
