Chapter 22 · Failure analysis

Failure analysis: from symptom to physical evidence

Failure analysis links an electrical failure signature to a physical location and, when possible, a physical mechanism. It is an evidence-building process: preserve the sample, reproduce and characterize the symptom, narrow the search region, choose an appropriate imaging or emission method, and expose the relevant structure carefully. No single microscope or image proves root cause. The methods here are an educational overview, not a laboratory procedure or service-provider workflow.

  • Organize observations by leakage, open, short, and intermittent signatures.
  • Understand how electrical localization guides microscopy and cross-section analysis.
  • Connect physical evidence back to PMIC circuit, device, process, and reliability hypotheses.

Build a traceable evidence path

Record the failing operating mode, supply and load conditions, temperature, timing, test setup, and whether the behavior is stable or intermittent. Compare failing and known-good units with the same measurement configuration. Preserve a reproducible baseline before stressing or opening the device; an induced or altered signature may otherwise obscure the original condition.

Electrical signature narrows the search

Observed symptomUseful first questionsCandidate areas (examples)
Excess leakageWhich terminal pair, bias polarity, temperature, and voltage dependence?Junction, gate dielectric, ESD clamp, contamination, or damaged interconnect
Open / high resistanceIs it permanent, load dependent, or mechanically sensitive?Contact/via, bond, fuse, current route, or cracked connection
Short / low resistanceWhich nodes are coupled, and does the path change with bias?Dielectric breakdown, metal bridge, protection structure, or package damage
Intermittent / driftDoes temperature, vibration, power cycling, or switching activity correlate?Marginal connection, thermal path, unstable device, or state-dependent circuit interaction

For a PMIC, characterize blocks and pins in a way that separates input, output, ground, enable, feedback, switching, and protection paths. For example, high supply current only when a buck switch is enabled suggests a different search than standby current that rises with temperature. These are prioritization clues, not diagnoses. A system-level fault can originate in the board, package, die, test setup, or interaction among them.

Localization before high-resolution imaging

Electrical probing can identify an anomalous path or compare candidate nets. Optical emission methods can reveal light associated with localized electrical activity. Time-resolved emission microscopy combines emission detection with timing information and can help investigate circuit operation; see the NIST program description. Emission depends on mechanism, bias, timing, and instrument sensitivity; a dark location does not establish that no defect exists.

Localization results guide a choice of physical method. Optical microscopy surveys the surface; scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examines morphology and local contrast; focused ion beam (FIB) milling can expose a targeted region; transmission electron microscopy (TEM) resolves very small structures in prepared samples. Methods differ in field of view, resolution, sample preparation, and destructiveness. The NIST semiconductor characterization and analytical technology resource provides broader context for characterization methods.

Do not jump from image to cause

A visible void, crack, or contrast feature may be incidental or may be an artifact of preparation. A causal claim is stronger when the physical finding is in the electrically implicated region, fits the observed signature, and is supported by appropriate controls or additional measurements.

Deprocessing and cross-section choices

Layer removal and cross-section preparation can expose buried structures but permanently alter the sample. The analyst must choose a target region and method based on the prior evidence, while accounting for alignment uncertainty and preparation artifacts. PMIC layouts can include thick metal, large power devices, deep wells, and distributed protection structures; a single cross-section may miss a localized defect or fail to show a three-dimensional current path. Preserve an unmodified reference sample where possible and document each preparation stage.

When physical findings suggest a process or reliability mechanism, compare them with electrical characterization and the intended stress history. A damaged interconnect near a high-current path is a hypothesis to test against route geometry, operating conditions, and known-good material—not automatically proof of electromigration. The analysis should end with a clear statement of what was observed, what remains uncertain, and what evidence would distinguish competing explanations.

Key points

  1. Start with a reproducible electrical signature and its operating conditions.
  2. Use localization to guide physical inspection rather than imaging blindly.
  3. Microscopy, emission, FIB, and TEM each answer different questions and have preparation limits.
  4. A sound conclusion links physical evidence to electrical behavior while documenting uncertainty and alternative causes.

Knowledge check

A cross-section shows a metal void near a PMIC power device. What is the strongest next step before calling it the root cause?

Physical evidence must explain the failing signature and fit the operating history. Correlation and appropriate controls help distinguish a causal defect from an incidental feature or preparation artifact.
