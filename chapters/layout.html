Chapter 19 · Physical implementation

PMIC layout, parasitics, and thermal design

A schematic does not show the resistance of a long metal route, the voltage induced by a fast-changing current, or the heat trapped beneath a power switch. In a PMIC, those effects can become part of the circuit. Layout must make high-current loops compact, keep sensitive analog nodes quiet, respect device isolation, and provide a path for heat to leave the die. The numbers below are first-order teaching models; extracted parasitics and package measurements are needed for product sign-off.

  • Estimate routing resistance and current-related voltage drop.
  • Recognize inductive switching overshoot and coupling paths.
  • Relate floorplanning choices to substrate noise and thermal spreading.

Current paths and IR drop

Current flows in a loop. For a buck converter, the hot loop includes the input bypass capacitor, high-side switch, low-side return path, and their connecting metal. Reducing loop area and keeping the bypass capacitor close to the switching devices can reduce parasitic inductance and radiated magnetic field. High-current routes also need enough width, contacts, and vias; a route that is electrically continuous can still create an unacceptable voltage drop or local heating.

$$R=\rho\frac{L}{wt},\qquad \Delta V=IR$$
For a uniform conductor, \(\rho\) is resistivity in \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm m\), \(L,w,t\) are length, width, and thickness in metres, and \(I\) is current in amperes. Real interconnect has temperature-, geometry-, and process-dependent resistance, plus contact and via contributions. The relation is a DC/low-frequency estimate; it does not model current crowding or electromigration limits.

Separate quiet reference and feedback routes from high di/dt switch-node routes where practical. Use local return paths and intentional Kelvin sensing when the control circuit must measure the voltage at a load or sense resistor. Layout rules for analog and mixed-signal ICs emphasize isolation, routing, and supply strategy; see Analog Devices AN-136, Layout Considerations. Its examples are general guidance, not a substitute for a process design manual.

Parasitic RLC and noise coupling

Every conductor has parasitic resistance and inductance; adjacent conductors and device terminals contribute capacitance. A first-order inductor model explains why a fast current edge can produce switch-node overshoot:

$$V_L=L\frac{dI}{dt}$$
With \(L\) in henries and \(dI/dt\) in amperes per second, \(V_L\) is volts. This is an ideal lumped-element estimate. Real ringing also depends on capacitance, damping, distributed interconnect, package parasitics, and the edge shape.

Large switching edges can couple through shared supply impedance, mutual capacitance, magnetic fields, or the substrate into references, error amplifiers, oscillator nodes, and protection circuits. BCD devices can occupy different wells and voltage domains; guard structures and substrate contacts help control coupling, but they consume area and cannot remove every parasitic path. Keep the switch node compact, avoid routing it under sensitive analog blocks, place decoupling near the load it serves, and check extracted or measured behavior at relevant corners.

Engineering check

If a reference ripple changes strongly with switch frequency or power-device placement, investigate shared return impedance and coupling before changing the reference circuit. Compare quiet and switching conditions, then inspect routes and substrate connections.

Heat flow and hot spots

Conduction loss, switching loss, and control overhead become heat. A simplified steady-state estimate relates power dissipation to temperature rise:

$$\Delta T\approx P_{\mathrm{diss}}\,\theta_{\mathrm{th}}$$
\(P_{\mathrm{diss}}\) is watts, \(\theta_{\mathrm{th}}\) is an effective thermal resistance in K/W, and \(\Delta T\) is kelvins (numerically equal to °C for a temperature difference). This lumped model assumes steady state and a defined boundary condition; transient heating, multiple heat paths, neighboring blocks, die attach, and package/board conditions require a thermal network or simulation.

A large power transistor may spread heat across its active area, but current crowding around contacts, narrow metal, or device edges can produce local hot spots. Thick top metal may improve power routing while changing stress and thermal spreading. Keep thermal estimates tied to the intended package and board, and assess device junction temperature under realistic load profiles. Electrical resistance itself rises with temperature for many metal conductors, so heating can increase IR drop and concentrate dissipation further.

Key points

  1. Current-loop geometry, conductor resistance, and contact placement affect efficiency and voltage accuracy.
  2. Fast current edges interact with parasitic inductance and capacitance; compact hot loops can reduce overshoot and coupling.
  3. Separate sensitive analog paths from noisy switching regions while respecting BCD isolation requirements.
  4. Thermal resistance is a boundary-condition-dependent approximation; validate the device, package, and board together.

Knowledge check

A PMIC feedback node shows ripple synchronized with a power switch edge. Which layout investigation is a useful first step?

Edge-synchronous ripple suggests a coupling path may exist. Review geometry, returns, substrate connections, and extracted or measured parasitics before changing the control loop.
