BCD Process Integration

A BCD platform combines low-voltage CMOS, bipolar devices, high-voltage DMOS, isolation, and passives. The process is a sequence of linked modules: each layer becomes the starting point for later implants, thermal steps, contacts, and interconnect. This chapter maps those dependencies, then uses the retained cross-section engine to inspect an illustrative low-voltage CMOS module that can sit inside a broader BCD flow.

  • Explain how a BCD process adds high-voltage and bipolar modules around a CMOS base flow.
  • Relate isolation, wells, drift regions, gate stacks, contacts, and metal to the devices they enable.
  • Inspect a simplified CMOS cross-section and distinguish its scope from a complete BCD process.
  • Use a long-channel threshold-voltage model to reason about channel doping and gate-dielectric capacitance.
  • Trace how thermal budget and process order affect earlier junctions and later interconnect.

How modules interact

Isolation and well boundaries

Shallow-trench or other isolation separates active device regions, while wells and optional deeper isolation regions establish body connections and voltage domains. The chosen structure affects parasitic paths, latch-up susceptibility, and available device combinations. See substrate and well choices, isolation etch, and oxide and field structures.

Low-voltage CMOS and HV device modules

A CMOS base module typically includes channel and well adjustment, a gate stack, source/drain formation, activation, and contacts. A high-voltage LDMOS adds a laterally extended drift region and field-management structures around the channel. The drift and isolation design must support voltage while controlling resistance and electric-field crowding; process dimensions and implant conditions are technology-specific. The stepper below demonstrates only the CMOS base module. Chapter 07 explains the qualitative LDMOS trade-off.

Bipolar and passive integration

BCD platforms may add vertical or lateral bipolar devices and passive structures in shared or dedicated process modules. Their junctions, resistors, and capacitors compete for area and interact with well/isolation and interconnect choices. The exact device menu varies by platform; see device fundamentals and passive devices.

Thermal budget, contacts, and interconnect

Early high-temperature steps can activate dopants and grow dielectrics; later heat can move dopants, alter junction profiles, or stress films. Contact and metal modules must connect power devices with acceptable resistance and current density. Thick top metal may help route current, but it does not remove local crowding, via resistance, or thermal constraints. See thermal budget, BEOL and top metal, and layout and thermal effects.

A first-order threshold-voltage model

Process integration targets electrical behavior, and MOS threshold voltage is one useful example. The long-channel NMOS expression below shows how substrate doping, gate-dielectric capacitance, work function, and fixed charge enter a simplified model. MIT OpenCourseWare's MOS electrostatics lectures provide the device-physics derivation. This expression is not a compact model for a short-channel power transistor or a process-control recipe.

$$V_t = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \frac{\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{Si}qN_A(2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}}, \qquad \phi_F = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_A}{n_i}, \qquad C_{ox} = \frac{\varepsilon_{ox}}{t_{ox}}$$
\(V_{FB} = \phi_{ms} - Q_f/C_{ox}\) is the flat-band term set by work-function difference and fixed oxide charge. This model assumes an idealized long-channel device and simplified charge distribution. Short-channel effects, interface states, mobility degradation, high-field behavior, and actual gate-stack details are omitted.

Process order is part of the design

Integration is constrained by module interactions. The exact order varies, but several questions must be resolved together:

  • Thermal budget: activation and dielectric formation must be balanced against later diffusion and film stress; HV drift and body-junction profiles can be sensitive to these steps.
  • Mask alignment and self-alignment: masks define device regions, while gates and spacers can provide local alignment for later implants. This influences overlap, resistance, and field distribution.
  • Isolation and latch-up paths: wells, guard structures, substrate contacts, and isolation determine parasitic current paths; the design must be checked at device and layout levels.
  • Contact and metal resistance: current density, via/contact resistance, and local heating matter for power devices. A thick top metal is one part of a wider current-path design.
  • Materials segregation: contamination-control rules constrain where some metals and materials may be processed. The exact fab controls are platform-specific.

Key points

  1. BCD integration builds a CMOS base alongside dedicated HV, bipolar, isolation, and passive-device modules.
  2. Wells and isolation establish device bodies, voltage domains, and parasitic-current boundaries.
  3. HV drift and field-shaping structures support voltage blocking while adding resistance and area trade-offs.
  4. Process order, thermal budget, contacts, and metal affect final electrical and reliability behavior.
  5. The stepper and threshold calculator are limited educational models, not a validated BCD process flow or TCAD.
