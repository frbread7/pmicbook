Chapter 03 · Linear regulation

References and LDOs

A reference establishes a voltage or current standard; a regulator uses feedback to make a power rail follow a target despite supply and load changes. This chapter builds the circuit intuition from a pass element, an error amplifier, and a feedback divider.

  • Trace the LDO feedback loop and identify its pass device.
  • Estimate dropout, dissipation, efficiency, and divider setting.
  • Explain why stability, transient behavior, and reference accuracy depend on implementation.

Reference, bias, and feedback

A voltage reference aims to be relatively insensitive to supply and temperature changes; a bandgap reference combines temperature trends of junction-based voltages to produce a more stable target. Bias circuits distribute currents and voltages to analog blocks. The reference is not the regulated output itself: an error amplifier compares a scaled output to the target and adjusts a pass device.

In a common feedback divider, \(V_{FB}=V_{OUT}R_B/(R_T+R_B)\). When feedback holds near \(V_{REF}\), the ideal set point is \(V_{OUT}=V_{REF}(1+R_T/R_B)\). Real accuracy also includes reference error, amplifier offset, resistor tolerance, leakage, and load-dependent effects. See TI application report SLVA079, LDO basics.

The LDO loop and its local device primer

The pass device supplies load current; the error amplifier senses output through a divider and changes pass-device drive. A MOS pass transistor uses a gate-controlled channel between source and drain. Its threshold, mobility, geometry, and operating region affect available current and voltage drop. A PMOS high-side pass element can be driven without a charge pump in many architectures, while NMOS implementations may require gate voltage above the input. A BJT pass element needs base current and has its own saturation/headroom behavior. Chapter 05: Device fundamentals develops the device models.

For a MOS element behaving approximately as a resistance, \(V_{drop}\approx I_{load}R_{DS(on)}\) is an intuition, not a universal dropout equation. In other regions or topologies, headroom depends on overdrive, drive limits, current, temperature, and control architecture. Junctions in the device structure can also become forward biased or enter breakdown outside intended operation; they are part of the protection and failure picture. TI's dropout report describes why dropout depends on load and implementation.

Feedback loop stability depends on amplifier dynamics, pass-device transconductance, output capacitance, capacitor ESR, and load. A simple DC model cannot establish phase margin or guarantee stability. Use the regulator's specified capacitor range and stability guidance.

Engineering trade-offs

  • Noise: reference and amplifier noise can matter for sensitive analog rails; filtering and bandwidth trade with transient response.
  • Quiescent current: lower bias can improve light-load battery life but may constrain bandwidth and response.
  • Thermal: at substantial input-output difference and current, pass-device dissipation grows quickly; check junction temperature and package thermal path.
  • Device/process: voltage rating, gate oxide, isolation, well structure, and parasitic paths connect LDO design to BCD. Continue to BCD technology and high-voltage devices.

Knowledge check

What does the LDO error amplifier primarily do?

The pass element carries the load; the amplifier compares feedback against a reference and controls the pass element.

Why is a single resistance model not enough to claim an LDO dropout specification?

The approximation captures one possible operating region but not the full pass-device and control behavior.
