Chapter 08 · BCD integration

Integrated passive devices

PMICs need more than transistors: reference dividers, compensation networks, decoupling, bootstrap storage, protection, and timing all use passive structures. Integrated passives save external parts and interconnect, but silicon area, tolerance, voltage dependence, parasitics, and substrate coupling determine which functions belong on die.

  • Estimate resistor and capacitor behavior from first-order geometry.
  • Compare resistor, junction, MOS, and MIM implementations.
  • Recognize why inductors and large capacitors often remain external.

Resistors: geometry and matching

A uniform resistor is described approximately by R = R_s (L/W), where R_s is sheet resistance in Ω/□ and L/W is the number of squares. A longer, narrower resistor uses more area and can increase parasitic capacitance and sensitivity to geometry. Diffusion, well, and polysilicon resistors offer different sheet resistance, voltage coefficient, temperature coefficient, matching, and voltage limits; the available layers are technology-specific.

$$R=\rho\,\frac{L}{Wt}=R_s\frac{L}{W}$$
Assumes a uniform film with current along L, width W, and thickness t. Contacts, bends, current crowding, temperature, and voltage-dependent properties can add error. Use a technology’s characterized sheet resistance for a real design.

Capacitors and diodes

MOS capacitor

Uses gate-to-semiconductor capacitance. Its capacitance can depend strongly on bias and operating region; useful as a compact capacitor when that variation is acceptable.

MIM capacitor

A metal-insulator-metal stack can provide a more nearly linear capacitor for analog and decoupling roles. Density, dielectric voltage coefficient, leakage, and breakdown are platform-dependent.

Junction capacitor / diode

A reverse-biased junction provides voltage-dependent depletion capacitance; the same structure may serve diode or protection functions, with leakage and substrate interaction to consider.

For an ideal parallel plate C = εA/d, so area scales with capacitance for a fixed dielectric. Real layouts include fringe fields, interconnect, and neighboring conductors. The ideal E = ½CV² energy relation is useful, but a capacitor’s permissible voltage and lifetime are set by its physical dielectric and design rules. A published example of MIM capacitors and inductors integrated in an advanced RF BEOL stack is described in this 2008 technical paper; its structures are specific examples, not a general BCD density or design rule.

Inductors and the integration boundary

On-chip spiral inductors can be valuable at high frequency, but they consume substantial area and incur metal resistance, substrate loss, and coupling. Their quality factor, self-resonance, and nearby switching currents affect behavior. Many power converters instead use an external inductor to obtain useful energy storage without dedicating a large die region. This is a system partitioning choice, not an absolute rule.

Passive values and parasitics connect directly to the layout and parasitics chapter; high-current routes and thermal paths are covered in contacts and metal. Their process structures belong in the BCD integration overview.

Key points

  1. Resistor value scales with sheet resistance and geometry; contacts and voltage/temperature effects add error.
  2. MOS, MIM, and junction capacitors differ in bias dependence, density, linearity, and leakage.
  3. Large energy-storage capacitors and inductors often remain external because of area and loss, while small analog passives integrate well.
  4. Real passive models and limits depend on the selected technology and layout.

Knowledge check

A design increases a resistor’s length while holding width and sheet resistance constant. What happens in the first-order model?

With R = R_s L/W, doubling length doubles the number of squares and resistance. The simple model omits contacts, bends, and process variation.
