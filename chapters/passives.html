Integrated passive devices

PMICs need more than transistors: reference dividers, compensation networks, decoupling, bootstrap storage, protection, and timing all use passive structures. Integrated passives save external parts and interconnect, but silicon area, tolerance, voltage dependence, parasitics, and substrate coupling determine which functions belong on die.

  • Estimate resistor and capacitor behavior from first-order geometry.
  • Compare resistor, junction, MOS, and MIM implementations.
  • Recognize why inductors and large capacitors often remain external.

Resistors: geometry and matching

A uniform resistor is described approximately by R = R_s (L/W), where R_s is sheet resistance in Ω/□ and L/W is the number of squares. A longer, narrower resistor uses more area and can increase parasitic capacitance and sensitivity to geometry. Diffusion, well, and polysilicon resistors offer different sheet resistance, voltage coefficient, temperature coefficient, matching, and voltage limits; the available layers are technology-specific. For a divider or current-sense bias, select a resistor layer by the required value and current first, then check voltage coefficient, self-heating, matching, and terminal limits in the design kit. At fixed target resistance and width, a lower sheet-resistance layer needs more squares and generally more area: 1 kΩ needs 100 squares at 10 Ω/□ versus 10 squares at 100 Ω/□. At fixed divider values and supply voltage, changing layers does not change ideal divider current; choosing larger resistor values reduces that current but makes leakage and parasitic capacitance more important.

$$R=\rho\,\frac{L}{Wt}=R_s\frac{L}{W},\qquad \frac{\Delta R}{R}\approx \alpha_R\Delta V$$
Assumes a uniform film with current along L, width W, and thickness t. Here \(\alpha_R\) is a local voltage coefficient in V⁻¹ around a stated bias point; real design kits may use a nonlinear or polarity-dependent model. Contacts, bends, current crowding, temperature, and voltage-dependent properties can add error. For example, a 1 kΩ target with a characterized 100 Ω/□ layer needs 10 squares before contact and layout corrections. This arithmetic illustrates geometry only; 100 Ω/□ is a hypothetical input, not a process recommendation. Use technology-characterized sheet resistance and matching data for design.

Capacitors and diodes

MOS capacitor

Uses gate-to-semiconductor capacitance. Its capacitance can depend strongly on bias and operating region; useful as a compact capacitor when that variation is acceptable.

MIM capacitor

A metal-insulator-metal stack can provide a more nearly linear capacitor for analog and decoupling roles. Density, dielectric voltage coefficient, leakage, and breakdown are platform-dependent.

Junction capacitor / diode

A reverse-biased junction provides voltage-dependent depletion capacitance; the same structure may serve diode or protection functions, with leakage and substrate interaction to consider.

For an ideal parallel plate C = εA/d, so area scales with capacitance for a fixed dielectric. In a PMIC, estimate a required decoupling capacitor from the transient charge: C ≥ I·Δt/ΔV. For example, supplying 1 mA for 1 μs while allowing 10 mV droop requires 100 nF in this idealized charge balance. If the design-kit areal capacitance is \(C_A\) in F/m² and the target capacitance is \(C_{\text{target}}\), the ideal active plate area is \(A\approx C_{\text{target}}/C_A\); routing, spacing, fringe fields and keep-outs increase the layout area. The example omits ESR/ESL, regulator response and load waveform, so it is not a decoupling prescription. Capacitor loss can be screened with \(P_{ESR}=I_{rms}^2R_{ESR}\).

Real layouts include fringe fields, interconnect, and neighboring conductors. The ideal E = ½CV² energy relation is useful, but a capacitor’s permissible voltage and lifetime are set by its physical dielectric and design rules. A local polynomial model is \(C(V)=C_0[1+\alpha_1(V-V_0)+\alpha_2(V-V_0)^2]\), where the first- and second-order voltage coefficients have units V⁻¹ and V⁻²; a real model may need more terms and is only valid over its characterized bias range. MOS and junction capacitance vary with bias; a MIM capacitor is often more linear, but its density, voltage coefficient, leakage and breakdown remain process-specific. The MOS C–V dependence follows the semiconductor surface charge and is described in this Tektronix/Keithley C–V application note. A published example of MIM capacitors and inductors integrated in an advanced RF BEOL stack is described in this 2008 technical paper; its structures are specific examples, not a general BCD density or design rule. For an analog pole or compensation zero, bias-dependent C can move the pole. Substrate-connected plates, junction caps and switching-node metal can inject displacement current \(i=C_{sub}\,dV/dt\) into the substrate; shielding or guard structures can reduce coupling into sensitive nodes but consume area and add parasitic capacitance. For switching-node or bootstrap storage, check maximum differential voltage, ripple current and coupling to substrate and nearby quiet nodes.

Inductors and the integration boundary

On-chip spiral inductors can be valuable at high frequency, but they consume substantial area and incur metal resistance, substrate loss, and coupling. Their quality factor, self-resonance, and nearby switching currents affect behavior. For a series-loss model, \(Q=\omega L/R_{series}\); higher Q means lower loss relative to stored magnetic energy at that frequency, while parasitic capacitance eventually causes self-resonance. A lossy substrate or nearby switching node can lower effective Q even when the metal geometry is unchanged. Many power converters instead use an external inductor to obtain useful energy storage without dedicating a large die region. Likewise, integrated capacitor area can dominate when the control function needs large energy storage: choose on-die passives for compact timing, compensation, sensing or local decoupling when parasitic and voltage limits fit, and compare external parts when area, ripple current or energy dominates. This is a system partitioning choice, not an absolute rule.

Passive values and parasitics connect directly to the layout and parasitics chapter; high-current routes and thermal paths are covered in contacts and metal. Their process structures belong in the BCD integration overview.

Key points

  1. Resistor value scales with sheet resistance and geometry; contacts and voltage/temperature effects add error.
  2. MOS, MIM, and junction capacitors differ in bias dependence, density, linearity, and leakage.
  3. Large energy-storage capacitors and inductors often remain external because of area and loss, while small analog passives integrate well.
  4. Real passive models and limits depend on the selected technology and layout.

Knowledge check

A design increases a resistor’s length while holding width and sheet resistance constant. What happens in the first-order model?

With R = R_s L/W, doubling length doubles the number of squares and resistance. The simple model omits contacts, bends, and process variation.
