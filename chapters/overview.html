Chapter 01 · System context

PMIC systems and power domains

A power-management IC takes energy from one or more sources and makes the voltage, current, timing, and protection behavior that a product’s subsystems need. To understand the chip, start with the system: its input sources, loads, operating states, and the consequences when a rail moves out of range.

  • Draw a power tree from the source through conversion and regulation to the loads.
  • Explain why one product often needs several rails and power domains.
  • Connect system needs such as sequencing and transients to measurable rail requirements.
  • Trace a rail requirement forward to circuit, device, process, and layout choices.

A rail is part of a system

A battery or adapter rarely connects directly to every load. A product can combine conversion stages, linear regulators, references, load switches, chargers, monitors, and protection. Some of those functions may share one PMIC; others may be separate devices. The partition depends on required voltage range, current, noise, efficiency, isolation, and product constraints.

Each named power domain groups loads with related supply, noise, and operating requirements. A sensor can need a quiet supply while a processor causes fast load changes. An actuator may draw much more current than either. Separate rails or local regulation can help manage those differences, but add conversion loss, silicon area, pins, sequencing logic, and design effort.

Engineering intuition

Start from the load and work backward. Ask what voltage range the load can use, when it turns on, how quickly its current changes, what disturbances it can tolerate, and whether it can be safely disconnected. Those answers become PMIC requirements.

Turn the product needs into rail requirements

A nominal voltage is not a complete specification. A rail is judged over input, load, temperature, time, and fault conditions. The terms below recur in the later regulator, switching, characterization, and reliability chapters.

System questionElectrical requirementWhy it matters
How much source voltage is available?Input range, UVLO, dropout or conversion ratioThe rail must start and stay in regulation across source variation.
How much current does the load draw?Continuous, peak, startup, and current-limit behaviorSets switch/pass-device sizing, metal path, package, and thermal demand.
How much voltage error is allowed?Accuracy, line regulation, load regulationDefines what the load sees as input or output current changes.
How quickly does the load change?Transient deviation and recoveryOutput capacitance, control-loop response, and interconnect impedance matter.
How much noise can the load tolerate?Ripple, output noise, PSRR, EMI limitsCoupling between switch nodes, substrate, rails, and sensitive circuits can affect function.
What happens in a fault or state transition?Sequencing, inrush, reverse current, OVP, thermal shutdownPower domains must enter and leave operating states safely.

The specifications are linked. Reducing the output-voltage error under a fast load step can demand more bandwidth, larger capacitors, or a different architecture. Increasing current can improve load capability but increase conduction loss and die area. Lowering switching noise can affect efficiency, size, and control complexity. There is rarely one best rail design independent of the system use case.

For a first power budget, use conservation of energy. If a load receives \(P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out}\), the converter input power is \(P_{in}=P_{out}/\eta\), where \(\eta\) is efficiency as a fraction. The difference \(P_{loss}=P_{in}-P_{out}\) must be dissipated or transferred elsewhere. Efficiency must be measured or modeled at a stated operating point; it is not one constant across all loads.

$$P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out},\qquad P_{in}=\frac{P_{out}}{\eta},\qquad P_{loss}=P_{in}-P_{out}$$
Units: volts × amperes = watts; \(\eta\) is dimensionless and must be between 0 and 1. This is a power-accounting identity, not a converter loss model.

Follow the requirement down to silicon

A rail requirement shapes more than a schematic. A high-side switch may need a device that tolerates a supply far above the core logic voltage. Isolation and wells keep different structures electrically separated. The selected process must support the desired transistors, passive devices, protection, and interconnect. Layout resistance and inductance then affect the delivered voltage, ripple, and heat.

BCD technology brings bipolar, CMOS, and DMOS capabilities onto a shared silicon platform; the actual device menu and voltage range depend on the specific technology. [STMicroelectronics, “BCD (Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS)”] For a PMIC, this can let precision analog, digital control, and higher-voltage power elements coexist. The trade is that integration rules, device options, voltage domains, thermal paths, and parasitic interactions must all be designed together.

A practical first-pass checklist

  1. List every input source and its minimum, nominal, and maximum operating conditions.
  2. For every load, record required voltage range, continuous and peak current, startup behavior, and off-state behavior.
  3. Mark which rails require sequencing, fault isolation, reverse-current blocking, or current limiting.
  4. Record transient, ripple, noise, thermal, and operating-lifetime constraints from the system specification.
  5. Compare candidate circuit types and note the device voltage, current, passive, package, and process requirements they create.
  6. Plan how each requirement will be characterized at bench, wafer, and production level.
Do not confuse a family label with a specification

“Automotive,” “low noise,” or “high voltage” does not define a universal numeric requirement. The product specification, operating conditions, applicable standards, and target technology determine the actual limits.

Key points

  1. A PMIC is best understood as a set of power functions serving a system’s loads and operating states.
  2. Rails must be specified over input, current, time, noise, temperature, startup, and fault conditions.
  3. Efficiency converts output power into an input-power and loss budget; losses create a thermal requirement.
  4. Circuit choice flows into device selection, BCD process integration, layout, characterization, and reliability.
