Semiconductor devices for PMICs

A power-management IC combines small-signal devices with transistors that must block voltage or carry substantial current. Device physics explains why a circuit’s ideal equations acquire limits: a switch has resistance, a junction leaks and breaks down, and every well boundary can host a parasitic path.

  • Use the long-channel MOS model as intuition, while recognizing its limits.
  • Explain basic BJT action and why bipolar devices can serve PMIC references.

Pn junctions: isolation and unwanted conduction

At a p-n boundary, mobile carriers diffuse and leave charged dopant ions, creating a depletion region and built-in electric field. Reverse bias widens that region and suppresses forward injection, but reverse leakage can persist or rise with voltage and temperature; forward bias injects carriers across the junction. These junctions isolate wells, form diodes, and appear inside MOSFETs. Breakdown is a device operating limit, not a safe clamp rating.

$$W \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A}+\frac{1}{N_D}\right)(V_{bi}+V_R)}$$
Abrupt-junction depletion approximation. \(W\) is in meters when \(\epsilon_s\) is F/m, \(N_A\) and \(N_D\) are m⁻³, and voltages are V. A one-sided approximation follows only when one doping concentration is much larger than the other. Graded profiles, high fields, and temperature-dependent generation change the result. See MIT OpenCourseWare's lectures on junction electrostatics and depletion capacitance.

MOSFETs: the PMIC workhorse

A gate voltage controls channel charge through a dielectric. In an enhancement NMOS, a sufficiently positive gate-to-source voltage forms an inversion channel. PMOS uses opposite polarities. A first-order long-channel saturation model is I_D ≈ ½ μ C_ox (W/L) (V_GS − V_T)²; it helps explain the effects of mobility, geometry, and overdrive, but ignores short-channel fields, velocity saturation, mobility degradation, series resistance, self-heating, and process variation. MIT OpenCourseWare's MOSFET lectures cover long-channel current-voltage behavior and body effect (Lectures 26–27); the equation here is a first-order teaching model.

In an LDO, the pass FET must support the input-output voltage difference while supplying load current. In a switching converter it is repeatedly driven on and off. Lower channel resistance can improve conduction loss, but often requires more device area, gate charge, and capacitance. The circuit context and switching frequency therefore matter as much as a transistor’s headline resistance.

BJTs: controlled carrier injection for analog functions

An NPN bipolar junction transistor has an emitter, a thin base, and a collector. In forward-active operation the emitter–base junction is forward biased and the collector–base junction is reverse biased. Carriers injected from the emitter cross the base and are collected, so a comparatively small base current can accompany a larger collector current. The simplified relation \(\beta=I_C/I_B\) defines current gain; \(\beta\) varies with current, temperature, geometry, and technology and is not a fixed PMIC design constant. PNP devices reverse the polarities. These operating conditions and limits are developed in MIT OpenCourseWare's BJT lectures 34–36.

$$I_C\approx I_S e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad V_T=\frac{kT}{q},\qquad \beta=\frac{I_C}{I_B}$$
Forward-active, low-injection teaching approximation. \(I_S\) depends on the device and temperature; \(V_T\) is thermal voltage, about 25.9 mV at 300 K. Series resistance, high injection, recombination, Early effect, and saturation are omitted. Never apply this expression as a full transistor model or a specified current-gain guarantee.

The temperature-dependent \(V_{BE}\) and the difference in \(V_{BE}\) between differently biased junctions help explain bandgap-reference concepts. A BCD platform may form bipolar emitter, base, collector, and isolation regions with shared or dedicated implants and thermal steps; exact structures and gain depend on the public device menu of the technology. See BCD process integration. A deliberately designed bipolar device is distinct from the unwanted parasitic bipolar path involved in latch-up.

Body effect, avalanche, and parasitic devices

For an NMOS, raising source voltage above body voltage increases reverse bias across the source-body junction and generally raises threshold voltage. The familiar long-channel relation is V_T = V_T0 + γ(√(2φ_F + V_SB) − √(2φ_F)). It assumes a bulk-like body and simplified depletion charge; short-channel electrostatics and nonuniform wells require more complete models. Isolated wells can permit body connections that reduce unwanted body bias, but isolation and latch-up constraints remain.

Reverse-biased junctions can conduct by avalanche at high electric field. MOS structures also contain parasitic bipolar transistors; coupled wells, substrate resistance, and injected current can trigger regenerative latch-up. Device spacing, contacts, guard structures, and well design are part of the protection strategy. See BCD architecture, HV/LDMOS devices, and the reliability chapter. The ESD Industry Council survey is useful context for latch-up terminology, but is not a universal trigger model or qualification rule (White Paper 5: Latch-up Survey Report).

Key points

  1. Junctions provide useful isolation and diode behavior, while also setting leakage and breakdown constraints.
  2. MOSFET equations are approximations; parasitic resistance, charge, field, and temperature shape real behavior.
  3. A BJT uses emitter injection and base transport for analog functions; current gain and \(V_{BE}\) depend on operating conditions.
  4. Body bias changes MOS threshold; parasitic bipolar paths connect device physics to latch-up prevention.
  5. Device dimensions are circuit tradeoffs among area, current, voltage capability, capacitance, and heat.

Knowledge check

Why can a larger power MOSFET reduce conduction loss but still hurt a switching design?

The switch is not an ideal resistor: increasing width can reduce on-resistance, but gate charge and capacitances also rise and cost drive energy and area.
