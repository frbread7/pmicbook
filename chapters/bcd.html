BCD technology architecture

BCD brings bipolar, CMOS, and double-diffused MOS capabilities together on one platform so a power IC can combine precision analog, logic/control, and voltage- or current-handling devices. “BCD” names a family of integration approaches, not one universal layer stack or process flow. Published vendor and educational overviews describe this integration motivation; details differ among technologies (STMicroelectronics, BCD overview; IEEE BCD tutorial).

  • Map circuit roles to bipolar, CMOS, and DMOS devices.

Why combine device families?

Bipolar

Useful for selected analog functions such as references, gain stages, and robust junction behavior. Device availability and performance depend on the platform.

CMOS

Efficient logic and analog control, from comparators and state machines to gate drivers. Different oxide and threshold options may serve distinct voltage domains.

DMOS

Drift-region and double-diffused structures support power switching. Voltage capability, on-resistance, area, and switching charge trade against each other.

The system-level win is integration: fewer external components and shorter interconnects can enable compact power solutions. The cost is a more complex substrate, isolation scheme, process sequence, and set of device compromises. Not every PMIC needs every device family.

A conceptual cross-section

Different parts may sit at different potentials. Junction isolation uses reverse-biased boundaries; deep wells can create nested regions for selected body connections and substrate-noise management. Triple-well is one common conceptual arrangement, but neither its availability nor exact structure is universal. Isolation design must also consider parasitic bipolar paths and latch-up; refer to the device foundations and the ESD Industry Council latch-up survey for context.

Designing across voltage domains

  • Low-voltage core: area-efficient logic and analog devices operate within limited gate and junction voltage ratings.
  • High-voltage domain: drift regions, field shaping, and spacing distribute electric field; larger devices often consume more area and carry more parasitic capacitance.
  • Isolation and protection: wells, guard rings, ESD paths, and substrate contacts help control current injection and coupling.
  • Thermal/current path: power devices create local heat; metal, contacts, and package determine how current and heat leave the die.

A platform can offer different gate-dielectric options for low-voltage and higher-voltage transistors. A thinner equivalent oxide can strengthen gate control and increase gate capacitance, but the allowed gate-voltage stress and leakage/reliability constraints differ from those of a thicker high-voltage option. A thicker dielectric alone does not create an HV power device: drift design, junctions, field shaping, and isolation still set voltage capability. The public ETRI BCD trench-gate report provides one technology-specific thick-gate-oxide example; MIT OCW MOS lectures explain oxide capacitance and field stress. Do not transfer either source's dimensions to another platform.

There is no single “best BCD node”: product voltage, switching frequency, analog accuracy, load current, protection, and die cost pull the integration choices in different directions.

Key points

  1. BCD integrates complementary device capabilities; it does not identify a single process recipe.
  2. Isolation enables multiple voltage domains while introducing parasitic and layout constraints.
  3. HV devices, precision analog, digital control, protection, and thermal paths compete for area and process complexity.
  4. Public technology descriptions are examples, not transferable foundry specifications.

Knowledge check

Why does a BCD platform need more than simply adding a high-voltage transistor to a logic process?

Integration is the challenge: devices share substrate and process modules, so isolation, parasitic control, protection, and routing must be designed together.
