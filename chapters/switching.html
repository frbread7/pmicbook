Chapter 04 · Switched energy transfer

Switching Converters

Buck and boost converters move energy through controlled switching and passive storage. Their ideal conversion ratios are useful starting points; real PMICs also manage losses, ripple, loop dynamics, current limits, layout parasitics, and heat.

  • Use ideal continuous-conduction-mode conversion ratios.
  • Estimate inductor ripple from voltage, inductance, and switching frequency.
  • Explain the role of the MOS switch, diode/synchronous rectifier, and junctions.

Buck and boost energy paths

A buck converter steps a higher DC input down. During the switch-on interval, the input supplies the inductor and load; during off-time, inductor current continues through a freewheel path. A boost converter stores energy in an inductor while its switch is on and transfers energy to the output when the switch opens. A diode or synchronous MOSFET provides the current path. Real topologies include control, startup, protection, and parasitic elements omitted from these descriptions.

The main switch is a MOS device: gate voltage controls its channel, while on-resistance creates conduction loss and transition time creates switching loss. Body and well junctions matter for isolation and current paths. Chapter 05 introduces these device structures; Chapter 06 and 07 connect them to BCD and high-voltage design.

Ideal CCM models

In continuous conduction mode (CCM), an ideal buck has \(V_{out}/V_{in}=D\), where \(D\) is switch duty ratio. An ideal boost has \(V_{out}/V_{in}=1/(1-D)\). These steady-state ratios assume ideal switches and passive components, periodic operation, and CCM; they are not efficiency or transient predictions.

\[\Delta I_L^{buck}\approx\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_s},\qquad\Delta I_L^{boost}\approx\frac{V_{in}D}{Lf_s}\]

Here \(L\) is inductance in henries, \(f_s\) is switching frequency in hertz, and ripple is peak-to-peak amperes. The estimates omit winding resistance, switch drops, ripple coupling, control mode, and boundary/discontinuous conduction. TI's buck power-stage report presents a calculation method for an integrated-switch converter in CCM; its boost-converter report discusses operation and practical duty-ratio limits. Neither report validates this ideal simulator for a particular PMIC or controller.

What the ideal model leaves out

  • Losses: switch conduction, switching transitions, gate drive, inductor copper/core, and diode losses lower efficiency and raise temperature.
  • Operating mode: at light load, many regulators enter discontinuous, pulse-skipping, or burst operation; CCM equations no longer describe the waveform fully.
  • Current path and layout: fast switching loops create parasitic inductance and noise; placement, return path, grounding, and metal resistance affect ringing and emissions.
  • Device/process: breakdown margin, drift-region resistance, isolation, parasitic bipolar action, and thermal limits constrain integrated power stages.

For outputs that exceed or invert the source, a charge pump may avoid a magnetic component at modest power, with ripple and conversion losses that depend on load and switching conditions. See Analog Devices' charge-pump guide. Continue to layout, parasitics, and thermal constraints.

Power paths, chargers, and protection

A PMIC power path decides how an input source, a battery, and system loads share energy. A charger regulates battery current and voltage according to the cell chemistry and its specified charge policy. Many single-cell lithium-ion chargers use a preconditioning phase for a deeply discharged cell, a controlled-current phase, then a constant-voltage phase followed by charge termination; temperature monitoring and safety timers may also be part of the device. These are common examples, not a universal profile for every chemistry or charger.

A power-path charger can feed the system rail from the adapter while managing battery charge separately. This lets a system start with a depleted battery and can prevent the load from confusing charge termination. The power-path FETs and control loops determine source priority, battery supplementation, and transition behavior; consult the actual charger data sheet and application conditions. See TI's application report on standalone linear power-path chargers.

A load switch is a controlled series MOSFET used to connect or isolate a rail. A controlled turn-on can reduce capacitive inrush and rail droop; current limiting or short-circuit protection responds to overload; reverse-current blocking can prevent one source or rail from feeding another. These features have distinct fault triggers and responses, which may include current clamp, retry, latch-off, or thermal shutdown. See TI's load-switch overview; feature behavior varies by device.

UVLO prevents operation when a supply is too low for valid control or power-stage behavior. OVP detects an input or rail above its permitted window and may disable a switch or disconnect the source. Threshold accuracy, hysteresis, delay, and recovery behavior are design-specific. Thermal shutdown is a last-resort protection response to excessive junction temperature; it does not replace thermal design, current limiting, or safe operating-area checks. Analog Devices' CN0190 circuit note illustrates one implementation of overvoltage, undervoltage, and overcurrent detection and protection.

Engineering check: draw the normal and fault current paths for adapter-only, battery-only, charging, startup, overload, reverse-current, undervoltage, overvoltage, and overtemperature states. Then check that the FET, interconnect, battery, and thermal limits remain valid for each state. A block diagram alone does not establish safe operation.

Knowledge check

In ideal CCM, a buck converter receives 12 V and has duty ratio 0.25. What output voltage does the simple ratio predict?

For an ideal buck, \(V_{out}=D V_{in}=0.25\times12=3\,\mathrm{V}\).

What happens to the ideal buck inductor ripple estimate if switching frequency doubles while other values stay fixed?

The first-order ripple is inversely proportional to \(f_s\), assuming the same CCM operating point and idealized waveform.

What is a key purpose of a charger with a separate system power path?

The system rail and battery charge path can be managed separately, but the actual charger behavior and cell constraints remain device- and chemistry-specific.

Which statement about thermal shutdown is sound?

Thermal shutdown is generally a last-resort protection behavior; design still needs margin for normal operating and fault conditions.
