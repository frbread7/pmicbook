PMIC interconnect: contacts, BEOL and reliability

Power-management ICs rely on contacts, vias, and metal to connect control circuits with high-current devices and package terminals. Resistance causes IR drop and self-heating; local current density and temperature affect interconnect reliability. The retained ProcessBook models explain deposition, fill, line scaling, and relative stress trends. They are illustrative, not a foundry stack, design rule, or lifetime prediction.

  • Trace current from a power device through contacts, vias, upper metal, and package connections.
  • Explain why copper damascene uses patterned dielectric, barriers, fill, and CMP.
  • Use the retained fine-line models to distinguish local signal RC from PMIC supply-path IR drop.
  • Compare relative electromigration stress trends without inferring a product lifetime.

From subtractive aluminum to copper damascene

Aluminum interconnect was commonly patterned by subtractive etching. Copper has lower bulk resistivity, but is difficult to pattern with conventional volatile-product plasma etches and can diffuse into surrounding dielectrics and silicon. Copper interconnect therefore uses a damascene approach with a diffusion barrier.

In damascene integration, dielectric trenches and contact vias are patterned first, lined with a barrier, filled with metal, and planarized by CMP. Dual damascene forms a via and its connected line in a shared metal-fill sequence. Exact stacks and process order vary by technology.

Contacts, vias and thick top metal in a PMIC

Power-device source, drain, and body contacts feed local metal, vias, and upper-level buses. Contact and via resistance can concentrate voltage drop and heating at current bottlenecks. Wider or thicker upper metal can reduce line resistance, but consumes routing area and changes capacitance, stress, and thermal spreading. The right trade-off depends on the current waveform, layout, stack, and package return path.

$$\Delta V = IR,\qquad P_{\mathrm{loss}}=I^2R,\qquad J=I/A$$
These relations are first-order electrical estimates. Current crowding at contacts and corners, temperature-dependent resistivity, parallel paths, and self-heating require layout-aware analysis. No universal current-density value guarantees electromigration lifetime.

Why bottom-up fill matters

A conformal deposit can close a narrow opening before its interior fills, leaving a seam or void. In one electrochemical copper-fill approach, suppressor, accelerator, and leveler additives change local deposition rates so the recessed region fills before the opening closes. This explains the engineering objective; actual additive chemistry and feature dimensions are process-specific.

Fine-line scaling as a comparison case

At very small line widths, surface and grain-boundary scattering can raise effective resistivity while a barrier reduces the available conductor area. The retained model below compares this fine-line case for copper and alternative metals. It is useful for understanding why geometry matters, but its 6–100 nm widths describe a local-interconnect scaling exercise, not a PMIC top-metal design rule. Power paths are usually evaluated with their actual wider conductors, contacts, vias, parallel routes, and temperature profile.

$$w_c=w-2t_b,\quad h_c=h-t_b,\quad \rho_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\rho_0\left(1+\frac{C\lambda}{\min(w_c,h_c)}\right),\quad R'=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}}{w_ch_c}$$
\(\lambda\) is an illustrative electron mean free path, \(C\) lumps scattering effects, \(t_b\) is barrier thickness, and \(w_c,h_c\) are conducting dimensions. The expression requires \(w_c>0\) and \(h_c>0\); the widget's input bounds enforce this. Material values and barrier assumptions are teaching inputs, not a process comparison or selection rule.

Signal RC delay versus power-path voltage drop

A distributed signal wire accumulates resistance and capacitance with length, so its first-order RC delay grows roughly with \(L^2\). Dielectric constant and neighboring geometry affect coupling. The retained fine-line widget explores that signal-path question. A PMIC power bus has a different primary concern: DC and pulsed current create voltage drop, local heating, and switching-loop parasitics; use Chapter 19 for current-loop and thermal interpretation. Lower-k materials can reduce capacitive coupling, while their mechanical and integration properties still matter.

$$\tau \approx 0.38\,R'C'L^2, \qquad C' \approx \varepsilon_0 k\left(2\frac{h}{s} + 2\frac{w}{t_{\text{ILD}}}\right) + C_{\text{fringe}}$$
\(R'\) and \(C'\) are resistance and capacitance per unit length, \(s\) is line spacing, and \(t_{\text{ILD}}\) is interlayer-dielectric thickness. The coefficient and capacitance geometry are illustrative; driver/load delay, return path, distributed inductance, and nonlinear switching are omitted.

Electromigration: relative stress trends

Current and temperature can drive atomic transport, forming voids or hillocks that change route resistance. Black’s equation is an empirical stress model. Its constants depend on the material, interface, geometry, waveform, process, and failure criterion. This retained widget now compares ratios within one assumed mechanism; it does not report years of life or a qualification result.

$$\mathrm{MTTF} = A\,J^{-n}\exp\!\left(\frac{E_a}{kT}\right)$$
\(J\) is current density, \(n\) is an empirical current exponent, \(E_a\) is an effective activation energy, and \(A\) includes calibration factors. Ratios cancel \(A\) only when the assumed mechanism and parameters remain applicable at both points. Interconnect choices in PMICs

  • Power routes: size upper metal, contacts, and vias for the specified current waveform, IR drop, temperature rise, and local crowding. Parallel paths need their actual current distribution checked.
  • Return paths: place high-di/dt supply and return loops to limit parasitic inductance and coupling into sensitive analog nodes; see Chapter 19.
  • Package connection: bond wires, bumps, leadframes, and package planes add resistance, inductance, and thermal impedance. The die metal alone does not set system behavior.
  • Advanced options: hybrid bonding and backside routing have different integration, alignment, and thermal requirements. They are bounded context in Chapter 23, not default PMIC stack assumptions.

Key takeaways

  1. PMIC contacts, vias, upper metal, and package connections form a current and thermal path; local bottlenecks matter.
  2. Damascene patterns dielectric trenches and vias before metal fill, then planarizes with CMP.
  3. Fine-line scattering and barrier area can raise resistance; the retained scaling models are illustrative local-interconnect examples.
  4. Distributed signal RC can scale approximately with \(L^2\); supply paths also require IR-drop, switching-loop, and thermal analysis.
  5. Black-law ratios show sensitivity to assumed stress parameters but cannot determine a qualified lifetime without measured calibration.

Knowledge check

Why does a typical copper damascene flow pattern the dielectric first?

Damascene creates the recess in dielectric, adds barrier/fill, then uses CMP to remove overburden.

What is the distributed RC delay if the wire length is tripled?

Since both R and C are proportional to the length, τ ∝ L².

Within one illustrative Black-law parameter set, how does the relative trend factor change when current density triples and \(n=2\)?

The relative factor follows \(J^{-2}\), so tripling current gives \(3^{-2}=1/9\). This is not a prediction of field life.

Why can a PMIC metal route still fail a voltage-drop target when its wide upper bus appears adequate?

A power path is a series and parallel network. Analyze the actual device-to-package current path, including local bottlenecks.
