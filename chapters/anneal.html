Chapter 15 · Thermal budget

Annealing, activation and diffusion

Ion implantation places dopants and creates lattice damage. Annealing repairs part of that damage and moves dopants onto electrically active lattice sites, but heat also redistributes them. For PMIC and BCD devices, this thermal history influences junction depth, well and drift profiles, leakage, resistance and device-to-device variation.

  • Use Fick’s laws and Arrhenius diffusivity to describe dopant redistribution.
  • Compare constant-source and finite-dose diffusion profiles.
  • Relate an entire temperature-time history to an illustrative diffusion budget.
  • Explain why activation and diffusion impose competing anneal requirements.

Diffusion and activation

Atoms move through a crystal with the help of point defects such as vacancies and self-interstitials. The dominant mechanism depends on the dopant, temperature, concentration and defect population. Fick’s first law describes flux down a concentration gradient, and the second law describes how the profile evolves over time.

$$J=-D\frac{\partial C}{\partial x},\qquad \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial^2C}{\partial x^2},\qquad D=D_0e^{-E_a/kT}$$
The Arrhenius relation is a dilute, equilibrium-style approximation. Real implanted and highly doped profiles can be concentration- and defect-dependent. Diffusion length scales as \(\sqrt{Dt}\); it is not itself a predicted junction shift.

Annealing also repairs implantation damage and activates dopants by placing them on electrically useful lattice sites. The active fraction depends on species, concentration, damage, temperature and time. A thermal model alone cannot predict it.

Two ideal diffusion profiles

A constant surface concentration gives an erfc-like profile; a finite dose spreads into a broader, lower Gaussian-like profile. Implantation followed by annealing is closer to a broadened finite-dose distribution, though real implant profiles may be skewed and develop transient enhanced diffusion.

$$C_{const}(x,t)=C_s\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right),\qquad C_{dose}(x,t)=\frac{Q}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}$$
These textbook solutions assume constant diffusivity and simple boundary conditions. They omit concentration dependence, clustering, segregation, interfaces and transient point-defect effects.

Arrhenius diffusivity and cumulative heat

Because diffusivity changes steeply with temperature, comparing peak temperature alone is misleading. Under a constant-diffusivity approximation, a non-isothermal sequence can be summarized by \(D_{\mathrm{eff}}t_{\mathrm{total}}=\int D(T(t))dt\). Multiple process steps contribute to the total thermal history.

Furnace, RTA, spike and millisecond anneals

Long furnace cycles provide a large thermal exposure. Rapid thermal and spike processes shorten the time near peak temperature; flash and laser approaches can create very brief high-temperature surface excursions. The tradeoff is not simply “hotter is better”: the process must activate dopants and repair damage without unacceptable redistribution, melting or film/device degradation.

Transient and non-ideal behavior

Implant damage can temporarily increase point-defect concentrations and accelerate diffusion, especially for some dopants. Oxidation can also inject point defects near an interface. At high concentrations, dopant interactions, clustering and incomplete electrical activation invalidate simple dilute Gaussian models. These effects explain why measured profiles and sheet resistance are needed alongside process simulation.

  • Transient enhanced diffusion (TED): excess interstitials after implantation can temporarily enhance diffusion.
  • Oxidation enhanced diffusion (OED): oxidation-generated defects can change dopant transport near the interface.
  • Activation and clustering: total chemical dose does not equal electrically active dopant concentration.

See Doping and well/drift implants for the implant-profile model and BCD process integration for the thermal sequence context.

Key points

  1. Fick’s laws and Arrhenius diffusivity provide a useful first model for redistribution.
  2. Constant-source and finite-dose boundary conditions produce different profiles.
  3. Thermal budget depends on the full temperature history, not only the peak.
  4. Activation, damage recovery, diffusion, clustering and segregation are coupled in real implanted structures.
  5. Simulator coefficients and outcomes are illustrative; verify with public literature and measured structures.

Knowledge check

For a finite dose under a constant-diffusivity model, what happens to peak concentration as the profile spreads?

Dose is conserved in the ideal model while the characteristic profile width grows approximately as \(\sqrt{Dt}\).

Why can two anneals with the same peak temperature produce different diffusion?

Diffusivity is strongly temperature-dependent, so the complete temperature-time curve matters.

What is the role of post-implant annealing?

Annealing serves several purposes but can redistribute dopants and does not guarantee full activation.
