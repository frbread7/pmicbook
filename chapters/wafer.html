Substrate, Epitaxy & Wafer Preparation

PMIC process integration starts with the substrate: crystal orientation, resistivity, defects, isolation and contamination all constrain the devices that can be built. This chapter follows silicon from crystal growth through wafer preparation, then connects those choices to the wells, drift regions and isolated devices used in BCD technologies. Numerical examples are representative educational approximations.

  • Explain how crystal growth and segregation affect substrate dopant uniformity.
  • Relate resistivity to doping and mobility, and interpret substrate and epitaxial-layer specifications.
  • Describe how crystal orientation and wafer preparation affect device and process integration.
  • Connect epitaxy, wafer flatness and defects to BCD wells and high-voltage device structures.
  • Distinguish common contamination classes and the purpose of cleaning steps.

Silicon Feedstock and Single-Crystal Substrates

Silicon for semiconductor wafers is refined from silica. Metallurgical-grade silicon is converted to trichlorosilane, purified by distillation, then decomposed to deposit high-purity polysilicon rods (the Siemens process). This electronic-grade polysilicon is the feedstock for growing single-crystal ingots.

$$\mathrm{SiO_2 + 2C \rightarrow Si + 2CO}, \qquad \mathrm{Si + 3HCl \rightarrow SiHCl_3 + H_2}, \qquad \mathrm{SiHCl_3 + H_2 \rightarrow Si + 3HCl}$$

However, polycrystals are a collection of small crystal grains, so there are defects at each boundary. PMIC devices also require a single crystal(Single crystal) whose atoms are aligned in one direction from start to finish.

Czochralski Growth and Dopant Segregation

Most silicon wafers are made by the Czochralski method (CZ). High-purity polysilicon and a controlled dopant are melted in a quartz crucible. A seed crystal with the chosen orientation is dipped into the melt, then slowly rotated and pulled upward. Silicon solidifies in alignment with the seed to form a single-crystal ingot. A narrow neck at the start helps reduce dislocations propagated from the seed.

As liquid silicon solidifies, some dopants preferentially remain in the melt. The equilibrium segregation coefficient is the ratio of solid-side to liquid-side concentration at the interface, \(k_0=C_s/C_l\). For \(k_0<1\), dopant accumulates in the remaining melt, so concentration can rise toward the ingot tail. Under the well-mixed-melt approximation, the Scheil equation describes this trend.

$$C_s(f) = k_0\,C_0\,(1-f)^{k_0-1}$$
\(C_0\) is the initial dopant concentration in the melt and \(f\) is the fraction of the melt that has solidified. The simulator uses illustrative selected coefficients B ≈ 0.8, P ≈ 0.35, As ≈ 0.3 and Sb ≈ 0.023; these are not a measured ingot dataset, and effective segregation depends on growth conditions. Some metallic impurities have very small coefficients and preferentially remain in the melt. Float-zone (FZ) growth uses a moving molten zone and does not use a quartz crucible.

A CZ crystal contains 10¹⁷~10¹⁸ cm⁻³ of oxygen dissolved from a quartz crucible. Oxygen mechanically hardens the wafer and also plays an internal gettering role by forming precipitates inside the wafer during heat treatment to trap metal impurities. For power devices or detectors where oxygen is a problem, FZ(Float zone) wafers, which are melted without a crucible, are used, but it is difficult to grow them to 300 mm.

Resistivity and Dopant Concentration

Wafer specifications commonly identify conductivity type, orientation and resistivity. Resistivity is inversely related to carrier concentration times mobility, and provides an indirect check on substrate or epitaxial-layer doping.

$$\rho = \frac{1}{q\,(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)} \approx \frac{1}{q N \mu(N)}, \qquad \mu(N) = \mu_{\min} + \frac{\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min}}{1 + (N/N_{\mathrm{ref}})^{\alpha}}$$
Mobility decreases as doping increases due in part to ionized-impurity scattering. This simplified Caughey–Thomas-style fit uses illustrative room-temperature silicon parameters: electrons \(\mu_{\max}\) ≈ 1414, \(\mu_{\min}\) ≈ 68.5 cm²/V·s, \(N_{\mathrm{ref}}\) ≈ 9.2×10¹⁶ cm⁻³, \(\alpha\) ≈ 0.71; holes 470.5, 44.9, 2.23×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, 0.72. The set is not a calibrated process or compensated-doping mobility model.

Crystal orientation, notch, and size

Silicon has a diamond-cubic lattice, and wafer orientation affects interface, oxidation and etching behavior. The (100) surface is commonly selected for MOS structures because it can provide a favorable Si/SiO₂ interface. BCD platforms may select substrate and epitaxial-layer orientations to meet the needs of integrated bipolar, CMOS and high-voltage devices.

From ingot to wafer

The ingot is ground to diameter, notched, and sliced into wafers with a diamond wire. Slicing leaves surface roughness and subsurface damage. Lapping, chemical etching, edge rounding and polishing remove damage and produce a mirror finish. Wafer flatness and surface finish are specified according to the intended process; requirements vary by product and technology.

Epitaxy, BCD substrates and SOI

An epitaxial silicon layer can be grown on a polished substrate to provide a controlled device layer. In BCD technologies, the substrate and epitaxial layer form part of the starting architecture for wells, isolation and high-voltage drift structures. Their polarity, resistivity, thickness and sequence are platform-dependent; this chapter assigns no foundry recipe. SOI(Silicon On Insulator) places a buried oxide (BOX) beneath a thin silicon layer. Smart Cut is one method for forming such a layer. SOI is used in some transistor and RF technologies.

Cleaning and Contamination Control

Common wafer-surface contaminants include particles (dust or equipment debris), organic residues (including resist residue), metals (such as Fe, Cu, Ni, Na and K), and native oxide that forms on exposed silicon. Cleaning is repeated at selected points in fabrication; its sequence and frequency depend on the process flow.

RCA cleaning traces to Werner Kern and David A. Puotinen's “Cleaning solutions based on hydrogen peroxide for use in silicon semiconductor technology,” RCA Review 31(2), 187–206 (1970) (JST bibliographic record). In a later first-person retrospective, Kern recounts the work's origins and identifies SC-1/SC-2 as “Standard Clean, Steps 1 and 2” (1983 Citation Classic). A NIST NanoFab wet-bench manual describes one facility's SC1/SC2 baths and use of an RCA sequence before furnace or deposition work; it is an equipment procedure, not a universal recipe.

An experimental study describes conventional SC1 as effective for particles and organic matter while noting possible metal deposition from contaminated chemistry; SC2 is used to remove surface metals carried into the sequence. The exact result depends on the species, solution and process conditions (Kolbesen et al., Microelectronic Engineering 45 (1999), 197–208).

Why does metallic contamination matter? Transition-metal impurities can introduce species- and charge-state-dependent deep levels in silicon, some near mid-gap, that promote carrier recombination. Even low bulk concentrations can reduce minority-carrier lifetime; mobile alkali ions such as sodium and potassium can shift MOS threshold voltage. Wet SC1/SC2 cleaning targets surface contamination and does not directly remove impurities already distributed through the silicon bulk. The MIT OCW lecture on transition-metal levels discusses species-dependent impurity states.

After wet cleaning, wafers are rinsed with ultrapure water (UPW; nominal resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm) and dried. Drying can leave watermarks, while capillary forces can cause pattern collapse in narrow, high-aspect-ratio structures. IPA-assisted or supercritical CO₂ drying can reduce these risks in suitable processes. Ozonated water, megasonic agitation, plasma and vapor HF are among other cleaning approaches used for specific materials and residues.

Cleaning Dilemma

Cleaning must balance residue removal against loss or damage to exposed films and structures. The acceptable window depends on materials, geometry and process; no single recipe applies to every technology.

Key Takeaways

  1. High-purity polysilicon is grown into a single-crystal ingot, commonly by the CZ method. Wafer diameter and thickness depend on the production platform.
  2. For dopants with segregation coefficient \(k_0<1\), concentration tends to increase toward the ingot tail in the well-mixed-melt model. Boron is more uniform than phosphorus or arsenic in the representative examples shown.
  3. Resistivity is \(1/(qN\mu)\) and mobility decreases at high doping. At the same concentration, the p-type resistivity is about 3 times that of the n-type.
  4. (100) silicon is widely used for MOS structures because it can provide a favorable Si/SiO₂ interface. Wafer flatness metrics such as TTV and SFQR affect lithographic focus control.
  5. Cleaning chemistry depends on residue and process module. Oxidative cleans target organics, dilute HF removes oxide, and acidic peroxide cleans target many metallic residues. Transition-metal contamination can introduce recombination centers or mobile charge.

Knowledge Check

In a CZ ingot doped with phosphorus (k₀ ≈ 0.35), when 75% of the ingot has solidified, about how much doping at that point is on the seed side?

\(C_s(f)/C_s(0) = (1-f)^{k_0-1} = 0.25^{-0.65} \approx 2.46\). The resistivity decreases accordingly.

Which step is mainly responsible for metal ion removal in RCA cleaning?

The hydrochloric acid in SC2 dissolves the metal by forming a water-soluble chloride complex ion. SC1 is responsible for particles and organic matter, and DHF is responsible for oxide film.

What is the biggest reason for using a (100) wafer for MOS transistors?

The (100) orientation can provide a favorable Si/SiO₂ interface for MOS structures. Oxidation rate also depends on orientation; this qualitative comparison is not a process specification.
