Chapter 02 · System to circuit

PMIC Fundamentals

A power-management IC turns a source with imperfect voltage, current, and energy into one or more controlled power domains. Its architecture is a chain of trade-offs: the system asks for rails and load behavior, circuits regulate energy, and device and process choices set electrical and thermal limits.

  • Distinguish conversion, regulation, sequencing, and protection.
  • Estimate efficiency, dissipation, dropout, and load regulation with units.
  • Trace an output requirement toward the devices and technology that constrain it.

Start with the power domains

A product may need a low-noise analog rail, a digital core rail, a high-current processor rail, and a battery-charging path. The PMIC partitions these loads, establishes startup and shutdown order, and protects against conditions such as undervoltage, overcurrent, and overheating. A power tree is a system map; it does not by itself specify regulator stability, transient response, or safe operating area.

Engineering question

For each rail, record source range, output voltage, steady and peak load, allowed ripple, startup sequence, accuracy, temperature range, and what happens during a fault. Those requirements guide whether to use an LDO, a switching converter, a charge pump, or a combination.

Useful first-order metrics

Efficiency and heat

Efficiency is useful output power divided by input power: \(\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}\). For a linear regulator, the pass device approximately drops \(V_{in}-V_{out}\), so \(P_{loss}\approx(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}\) when quiescent current is small. A switching regulator transfers energy in pulses; its conduction, switching, magnetic, and control losses depend on operating conditions. A high efficiency percentage does not guarantee a cool die: absolute loss and thermal resistance also matter.

Regulation and response

Line regulation describes output change as input changes; load regulation describes output change as load changes. Both require stated measurement ranges and conditions. A load transient also tests loop response and output capacitance. Quiescent current \(I_Q\) is current consumed by the regulator itself; at light load it can dominate system battery life even when heavy-load efficiency is high.

Dropout and the local device picture

An LDO needs enough input-to-output headroom to control its pass element. A simple long-channel MOS intuition is that the gate controls channel charge; a pass MOSFET operated as a source follower or in its resistive region needs voltage headroom, and its resistance rises with temperature and process variation. A BJT pass device has a different base-drive and saturation picture. Junctions also provide isolation and protection paths, but can conduct unexpectedly under forward bias. These are introductory circuit models; Chapter 05 develops MOS, BJT, and junction behavior, and Chapter 03 applies it to LDOs. TI's application note distinguishes dropout behavior by regulator implementation and operating conditions; there is no universal fixed dropout voltage. TI, Understanding LDO Dropout.

From requirement to silicon

  1. System: define rails, load profiles, sequencing, and fault behavior.
  2. Circuit: choose regulation/conversion and control strategy; budget ripple, response, and losses.
  3. Device: check voltage rating, current density, breakdown, gate drive, and parasitic paths.
  4. Technology and implementation: connect the device to BCD isolation and drift structures, then account for layout resistance, heat, test, and reliability.

Knowledge check

A linear regulator changes 5 V to 3.3 V at 100 mA, ignoring its own current. What is its approximate pass-device loss?

\((5-3.3)\,\mathrm{V}\times0.1\,\mathrm{A}=0.17\,\mathrm{W}=170\,\mathrm{mW}\).

Why can a regulator with good heavy-load efficiency still drain a battery at light load?

At light load, control and bias current can dominate the current delivered to the load, so compare IQ and light-load operating modes.
